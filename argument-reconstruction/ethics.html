Thinker's Arcade

Argument Reconstruction Activity

This activity lets you practice identifying premises and conclusions, distinguishing moral from nonmoral premises, and evaluating arguments. It follows Lewis Vaughn's guideline that moral arguments typically include at least one moral and one nonmoral premise.

Exercise 1

"Animal cruelty causes unnecessary suffering. Causing unnecessary suffering is morally wrong. Therefore, animal cruelty is morally wrong."


Premises:

  1. Animal cruelty causes unnecessary suffering.
  2. Causing unnecessary suffering is morally wrong.

Conclusion: Animal cruelty is morally wrong.

Argument Type: Deductive.

Evaluation: Valid and sound.

Moral Premise: Causing unnecessary suffering is morally wrong.

Nonmoral Premise: Animal cruelty causes unnecessary suffering.

Exercise 2

"Most philosophers teach logic. Dr. Lee is a philosopher. Thus, Dr. Lee probably teaches logic."


Premises:

  1. Most philosophers teach logic.
  2. Dr. Lee is a philosopher.

Conclusion: Dr. Lee probably teaches logic.

Argument Type: Inductive.

Evaluation: Strong and cogent if the premises are accurate.

Exercise 3

"Factory farming causes severe animal suffering. Causing unnecessary animal suffering is morally wrong. Therefore, factory farming is morally wrong."


Nonmoral premise: Factory farming causes severe animal suffering.

Moral premise: Causing unnecessary animal suffering is morally wrong.

Conclusion: Factory farming is morally wrong.

Exercise 4

"Genetic modification alters organisms profoundly. Therefore, genetic modification is morally wrong."


Missing a moral premise explaining why profound alteration is morally problematic.

Exercise 5

"All harm is morally unacceptable. Therefore, recreational hunting is morally unacceptable."


Missing a nonmoral premise clearly stating that recreational hunting causes harm.

Exercise 6

"All humans deserve basic respect. Therefore, John deserves basic respect."


This initially looks like a moral-to-moral argument lacking a nonmoral premise. The hidden premise is that John is human. Stating it explicitly aligns with Vaughn's criterion.

Exercise 7

"All unnecessary suffering is morally wrong. Therefore, torture is morally wrong."


The argument requires the implicit nonmoral premise that torture causes unnecessary suffering. Making it explicit resolves the counterexample.

Exercise 8

"All mammals are warm-blooded. Whales are mammals. Therefore, whales are warm-blooded."


Type: Deductive.

Validity: Valid.

Soundness: Sound.

Exercise 9

"Most ethics students pass exams. Maria is an ethics student. Maria will likely pass the exam."


Type: Inductive.

Strength: Strong if the premises are true.

Cogency: Cogent when the premises are factually accurate.

Exercise 10

"All birds fly. Penguins are birds. Thus, penguins fly."


Validity: Valid.

Soundness: Unsound because the first premise is false.

Exercise 11

"Nearly all philosophers are wealthy. Jane is a philosopher. Thus, Jane is probably wealthy."


Strength: Strong.

Cogency: Not cogent because the premise is false.

Reflective Prompt

Do you believe Vaughn’s criterion—that moral arguments require explicit moral and nonmoral premises—is always correct? Can you identify an argument that challenges or supports this claim?

Summary

Moral arguments with moral conclusions typically require both moral and nonmoral premises. Arguments missing one type of premise rely on hidden assumptions. Making those premises explicit helps evaluate validity, soundness, strength, and cogency.
